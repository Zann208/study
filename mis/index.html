0 Start here: what an information system is
In one lineAn information system collects, processes, stores and distributes information so people in an organisation can run it, control it and make decisions.

Every business runs on information: what was sold, what is in stock, who owes money, which customers are unhappy. An information system (IS) is the set of parts that turns raw facts into that information and puts it in front of the right people. An IS is never just the computers. It always has three dimensions: the organisation that uses it, the management that decides with it, and the technology it runs on.

Information technology (IT) is the hardware, software, data and networks. It is the platform. The IS is what the organisation builds on that platform to do its work.

Example: Doi Roast

To keep the ideas concrete, the topics follow Doi Roast, a Chiang Mai coffee company with about 40 staff. It buys green beans from hill-tribe farms, roasts them, sells to cafés across northern Thailand and ships bags to customers who order online.

1 Information systems in global business today
In one lineFirms invest in information systems to reach six strategic objectives, and understanding any IS means looking at the organisation, the management and the technology together.

Why information systems matter

Spending shows how central IT has become. Businesses spent about $4 trillion on IT worldwide in 2019. In 1999 private firms put 14% of their invested capital into IT; by 2013 it was 33%. Gartner forecasts $6.37 trillion for 2026 (up 14.2% on $5.57 trillion in 2025) and more than $7.35 trillion in 2027, driven mostly by AI infrastructure, data centres, software and cloud.

Firms use IS to sense and respond to customer demand as it changes, keep inventories low and work more efficiently. Supply chains run fast on just-in-time inventory. Markets keep shifting too: newspaper readership falls while online video and blogs grow, social networks give customers a public voice, and AI is changing what work looks like.

What is new in IS

ChangeExamplesWhat it means
New technologyCloud computing, big data, the mobile digital platform, AIComputing moves online as services (SaaS); firms mine huge volumes of web, email and social data; smartphones and tablets become the main platform; intelligent automation spreads.
New management usageOnline collaboration, business intelligence, virtual meetingsSocial tools and shared documents coordinate teams; dashboards give managers real-time performance figures; video calls cut travel time and cost.
Changing organisationsSocial business, teleworking, co-creation of business valueFirms use social platforms with employees, customers and suppliers; work happens away from the office (55% of US businesses run some remote work programme); value shifts from products to solutions and experiences built with networks of partners.

Two themes keep returning through the subject: collaboration and community, and data analysis that supports decisions.

The digital firm

A digital firm is one where nearly all important relationships with customers, suppliers and employees are digitally enabled. Its core business processes (hiring, developing products, fulfilling orders, planning marketing) run over digital networks, and its key assets (intellectual property, core competencies, financial and human assets) are managed digitally.

  • Information for key decisions is available any time, anywhere.
  • It senses and responds to its environment instead of simply producing and selling.
  • It is flexible and agile.
  • Time shifting: business is no longer limited to the 9-to-5 day. Space shifting: it is no longer tied to one physical location.

IS now helps decide and drive corporate strategy. Strategy and systems depend on each other: a change in strategy needs changes to hardware, software, databases and networks, and what a firm wants to do in five years is often limited by what its systems will let it do.

Six strategic business objectives

ObjectiveMeansExample
Operational excellenceMore efficient operations, so higher profitWalmart's Retail Link connects suppliers to every store: when a customer buys an item, the supplier knows to ship a replacement. Walmart earns about $28 of sales per square foot against Target's $23 and under $12 for others.

At Doi Roast, linking café stock levels to the roasting schedule is operational excellence; a coffee subscription box is a new business model; remembering each café's preferred roast is customer intimacy.

IT or IS? Data or information?

Information technology is all the hardware and software a firm needs. An information system is a set of interrelated components that collect, process, store and distribute information to support decision making and control.

Data are raw facts. Information is data shaped into a form that is meaningful and useful to people. A supermarket checkout scans millions of barcodes (data); analysed, they become sales totals and stock levels (information).

Three dimensions of an IS

Organisation. People, structure, business processes, politics and culture. Structure shows the division of labour, authority and responsibility: senior management makes long-range strategic decisions, middle management carries out the programmes and plans, operational management monitors day-to-day work. Work is split into business functions (sales and marketing, manufacturing and production, finance and accounting, human resources), each with formal rules. Culture is the set of assumptions and values most people accept ("customer service", "quality"); not everyone agrees with it, which leads to politics, and both affect any IS.

Management. Managers make sense of the business environment, make decisions and form action plans to solve problems. They allocate human and financial resources, lead, and create new products and ideas driven largely by new knowledge and information.

Technology. Hardware, software, data management, and networking and telecommunications. IT is the platform on which an IS is built: the enabler.

Review questions

Q1

How are information systems transforming business, and how do they relate to globalisation?

Cloud, mobile, big data and AI change how firms work: collaboration goes online, managers see real-time dashboards, work moves out of the office, and value is created with networks of suppliers and customers. The same networks let firms coordinate production, suppliers and customers across countries, so supply chains become global and collaborative.

Q2

Why are information systems so essential for business management today?

They are how firms reach the six strategic objectives. Strategy and systems are interdependent, and some systems are needed simply to survive.

Q3

What is an information system and what are its components?

Interrelated components that collect (input), process, store and distribute (output) information for decision making and control, with feedback. Its dimensions are organisation, management and technology; the technology part is hardware, software, data management and networking.

Q4

Which academic disciplines study information systems?

Technical: computer science, management science, operations research. Behavioural: sociology, psychology, economics. The sociotechnical view combines both.

2 Global e-business and collaboration
In one lineBusiness processes are how work gets coordinated; each business function has its own systems, enterprise applications join them up, and collaboration tools connect the people.

Business processes

A business process is the collection of activities needed to produce a good or service, supported by flows of materials, information and knowledge. It is the way work is coordinated. How well processes are designed and coordinated often decides how well the business performs, so a process can be a competitive strength or a weakness.

FunctionExample processes
Manufacturing and productionAssembling the product, checking quality, producing bills of materials
Sales and marketingIdentifying customers, making customers aware of the product, selling it
Finance and accountingPaying creditors, creating financial statements, managing cash
Human resourcesHiring, evaluating performance, managing benefits, training

Many processes are cross-functional. Fulfilling one order at Doi Roast:

  1. Sales receives a café's order.
  2. Accounting checks the café's credit.
  3. Production roasts and packs the beans.
  4. The order ships, perhaps through a logistics company.
  5. Sales handles after-sales service.

Something as simple as filling an order needs coordination across several functions.

Business process re-engineering

Studying and improving processes can bring large gains. New technology changes the process itself:

  • Manual steps become automated.
  • Sequential steps happen at the same time.
  • Many people access and share the same information.
  • Entirely new processes appear, such as downloading an e-book or a music track instead of buying a physical copy.
  • AI supports or performs steps.

Systems for each function

No single system covers everything. Each function has its own systems, and cross-functional enterprise systems sit on top.

FunctionSystems
MarketingInteractive marketing, sales force automation, CRM, sales management, market research and forecasting, advertising and promotion, product management
ManufacturingManufacturing resource planning (ERP), manufacturing execution systems, engineering systems: together computer-integrated manufacturing
Human resourcesStrategic (workforce planning, succession), tactical (labour cost analysis, training effectiveness), operational (recruiting, payroll, benefits)
AccountingOrder processing, inventory control, accounts receivable, accounts payable, payroll, general ledger
FinanceCash management, investment management, capital budgeting, financial planning

Marketing. Marketing plans, promotes and sells existing products in existing markets and develops new products and markets. Interactive marketing uses the internet for two-way exchanges with customers (chat, discussion groups, web forms, email, and now mostly social media). Targeted marketing aims online effort four ways: community (ads tailored to virtual communities), content (banners on relevant pages), context (ads matched to what the user is doing, like Google's smart ads) and online behaviour (cookies, link tracking). Sales force automation (SFA) lets sales staff work in the field: record sales, email clients, track progress. An integrated SFA solution follows the sale from inquiry to customise to commit to order.

Manufacturing. Manufacturers once built to stock and reordered when inventory fell below a threshold. Just-in-time inventory, build-to-order, end-to-end supply chain visibility, contract manufacturing and web-based collaboration changed plant management. Computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) aims to simplify production processes and product designs, automate production and business functions, integrate all production and support processes, and create flexible, agile manufacturing. Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) automates production through manufacturing execution systems, process control, machine control and robot control. A manufacturing system combines five resources: materials, equipment, personnel, instructions and production facilities.

Human resources. Recruiting happens online, employee records live in databases, and an HR management system (HRMS) tracks hiring, job changes, pay changes and terminations. Skills inventories help assign people well; the same data supports training plans, computer-based appraisals and succession planning.

Accounting and finance. Order processing captures customer orders and feeds inventory and receivables. Inventory control records stock changes and produces shipping and reorder information. Accounts receivable records what customers owe and produces invoices and statements. Accounts payable records purchases from and payments to suppliers. Payroll records work and pay. The general ledger consolidates the others into financial statements. Finance systems forecast and manage cash, manage investment portfolios, evaluate the risk and return of capital spending, and forecast financing needs.

Collaboration

Collaboration is working with others towards shared, explicit goals. It matters more than ever because:

  • The nature of work changed. Stages of production once happened separately and passed work on; now they interact constantly.
  • Professional work grew. More jobs, often in services, need education, shared information and exchanged opinions; each person has expertise but must work with others.
  • The firm is organised differently. Strict hierarchy gave way to teams that set their own methods while senior managers measure results; power and decisions are decentralised.
  • The firm's scope widened. Production and development are spread across the globe.
  • Innovation is emphasised. New ideas more often come from groups building on each other's ideas than from lone geniuses.
  • Work culture changed. Diverse teams produce better results faster, people prefer teamwork, and firms tap the crowd (crowdsourcing).

Social media add conversations, transparency and immediate positive or negative feedback, letting firms sense and respond from design through delivery. Tools include email and messaging, wikis, virtual worlds, virtual meeting systems and telepresence, and cloud suites with shared storage such as Google Workspace.

The IS function

The IS department is made up of programmers, systems analysts (who link the IS group with the rest of the business) and IS managers, led by a CIO (chief information officer). Many firms also have a CSO (security), CPO (privacy) and CKO (knowledge).

Case files

Three cases on ethics, security and building systems, each with the ideas needed to analyse it and an answer outline.

C1 Ethics: a social network's privacy record
In one lineA business model built on users' personal data keeps colliding with privacy, safety, competition and copyright, and ethical analysis gives a structured way to judge it.

The case in brief

  • Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp) earns most of its money from advertising built on personal data, so user privacy and revenue pull against each other.
  • Beacon (2007) posted users' purchases on other sites to their friends' feeds without permission; after a petition of 50,000+ users it was withdrawn.
  • The FTC acted twice: a 2012 order to stop deceiving users about privacy controls, then a record $5 billion fine in 2019 for breaking that order.
  • Cambridge Analytica: a quiz app collected data on its users and their friends, building a database of more than 87 million people that was used to target political messaging in the 2016 US election.
  • Data-sharing deals with at least 60 device makers gave access to users' friends' data without consent. Settlements include $650 million (facial recognition, Illinois) and $725 million (class action).
  • Under the EU's GDPR: a record €1.2 billion fine in 2023 for moving EU data to the US, plus earlier fines for children's data, forced consent to personalised ads and a breach affecting 530 million users.
  • Internal documents showed the firm knew Instagram could harm teenage girls' mental health and that a feed change rewarded outrage, yet did little.
  • The FTC called the Instagram and WhatsApp purchases moves to crush competition; the EU is investigating under its Digital Markets Act.
  • Its AI assistant was rolled out in all apps with no way to turn it off, gave made-up answers, was trained on public user posts with no easy opt-out, and reportedly on copyrighted books used without permission.
  • The firm points to its ESG reporting, but its model still depends on largely unrestricted use of personal data.

Ideas to apply

Five moral dimensions of the information age: information rights and obligations (privacy), property rights and obligations (intellectual property), accountability and control, system quality (data and system errors) and quality of life (social and mental effects).

Basic concepts. Responsibility: accepting the costs, duties and obligations of decisions you make. Accountability: mechanisms exist to find who took an action. Liability: laws let people recover damages done to them. Due process: laws are known and applied fairly, with a right of appeal.

Ethical analysis in five steps:

  1. Identify and describe the facts clearly.
  2. Define the conflict or dilemma and the higher-order values involved (privacy, freedom, protection of property).
  3. Identify the stakeholders.
  4. Identify the options you can reasonably take.
  5. Identify the likely consequences of each option.
Candidate principleTest
Golden RuleDo unto others as you would have them do unto you.
Kant's categorical imperativeIf an action is not right for everyone to take, it is not right for anyone.
Slippery slope (Descartes' rule of change)If an action cannot be taken repeatedly, it is not right to take at all.
Utilitarian principleTake the action that achieves the higher or greater value.
Risk aversion principleTake the action that produces the least harm or the least potential cost.
No free lunchAssume almost all tangible and intangible objects belong to someone unless stated otherwise; if someone created something useful to you, they probably want compensation.

Fair information practices (the basis of most privacy law, including GDPR): notice of what is collected, choice and consent, access to your own data, security of the data, and enforcement. GDPR adds explicit opt-in consent, the right to be forgotten, and limits on moving data out of the EU. ESG (environmental, social and governance) judges a company on more than profit and loss.

Questions

Q1

Pick one ethical dilemma from the case and analyse it.

Example: Cambridge Analytica. Facts: a quiz app took data on its users and their friends without the friends' knowledge, and the data was sold for political targeting. Dilemma: users' information rights versus the firm's revenue from an open developer platform. Stakeholders: users, their friends, app developers, advertisers, voters, regulators, shareholders. Options: allow broad developer access; restrict access to consenting users only; audit apps and ban misuse. Consequences: open access grows revenue but led to fines and lost trust; restriction costs some ad and developer value but protects users. Principles: it fails the Golden Rule and Kant's imperative (no one would accept their data being taken this way), and risk aversion favours restriction.

Q2

Can Meta run a successful business without invading privacy?

Arguments for: contextual ads based on page content rather than personal profiles, opt-in personalisation, subscriptions (Meta now sells ad-free subscriptions in the EU), and trust as a competitive advantage. Arguments against: revenue depends on precise targeting, and less data means lower ad prices. A defensible answer: yes, but at lower margins, with consent-based data use and privacy by default.

Q3

What ethical issues arise in developing and using its AI?

Property rights (training on copyrighted books and on users' posts without permission or payment: the no-free-lunch rule), information rights (no easy opt-out from data scraping), system quality (hallucinated answers, such as claiming to have a child), quality of life (misinformation, no option to turn the assistant off) and accountability (who is responsible when the AI misleads).

Q4

What is the ESG movement and how might it affect Meta's future?

ESG rates firms on environmental, social and governance performance. Investors increasingly screen on it, regulators lean on it, and employees and users care about it. Poor social scores on privacy, teen safety and misinformation could raise Meta's cost of capital, invite tougher regulation and push it to change its data practices.

C2 Security: ransomware stops a health payment network
In one lineOne missing control (multifactor authentication on a remote access tool) let a ransomware group shut down a system the whole US health sector depends on.

The case in brief

  • Ransomware encrypts files or locks a system and demands payment, usually in cryptocurrency, for the decryption key.
  • Change Healthcare, owned by UnitedHealth Group since 2022 ($8 billion), processes claims, payments and prescriptions for more than 67,000 pharmacies and handles about 15 billion claims ($1.5 trillion) a year, roughly a third of all US patient records.
  • February 2024: an affiliate of the BlackCat/ALPHV group, which sells ransomware as a service, got in through a remote access application without multifactor authentication, stole a reported 6 TB of data and encrypted systems.
  • The company disconnected more than 110 services. Eligibility checks, prior authorisations, claims and prescriptions stalled across hospitals, doctors, pharmacies and patients, and providers' cash flow dried up.
  • It paid a reported $22 million in Bitcoin. The affiliate said it was never paid, kept the data, and another group (RansomHub) later demanded a second ransom.
  • UnitedHealth advanced more than $3 billion to providers, criticised as too little. Recovery took over a month; class actions and a federal investigation followed.
  • Healthcare is among the most attacked critical infrastructure: ransomware hit 46 hospital systems in 2023, up from 25 in 2022.

Ideas to apply

DimensionIn this case
PeopleSet security standards and guidelines
OrganisationPut the standards into practice; promote best practice
TechnologyRansomware, cryptocurrencies, decryption keys
Business challengeLarge, complex infrastructure; many access points; uneven security
SolutionPrevent unauthorised access; back up and recover systems; protect critical infrastructure

Security is the policies, procedures and technical measures that prevent unauthorised access, change, theft or damage. Controls are the methods and standards that protect assets, keep records accurate and make sure management's standards are followed.

  • Framework: risk assessment, a security policy and acceptable use policy, identity management, disaster recovery and business continuity planning, regular IS audits.
  • Tools: multifactor authentication, firewalls, intrusion detection, anti-malware, encryption, patching, network segmentation, tested offline backups, an incident response plan.

Questions

Q1

How did the attack's impact reach beyond Change Healthcare's own clients?

Because it sits in the middle of the system, the outage stopped claims and prescriptions for hospitals, doctors and pharmacies that had no direct link to it, cut providers' income, delayed patients' care and medicines, exposed millions of people's health and identity data, and showed how one weak point can disrupt national critical infrastructure.

Q2

How could it have been prevented?

Multifactor authentication on every remote access point; regular risk assessments and audits after the acquisition; least-privilege access and network segmentation so one entry does not reach everything; monitoring and intrusion detection to catch data being copied out; tested offline backups and a practised incident response plan to limit downtime.

Q3

Is paying the ransom an effective solution?

Mostly no. There is no guarantee the data is deleted (here it was not), payment funds and encourages more attacks, the same data can be ransomed again, and some payments break sanctions law. Paying can shorten an outage when there is no backup, which is the argument for good backups instead.

C3 Building systems: process mining and business process management
In one lineProcess mining reads the event logs a firm's systems already keep to show how processes really run, so inefficiencies can be found and fixed in weeks rather than months.

The case in brief

  • Celonis (Munich and New York, 3,000+ staff, 1,400+ customer companies) makes process mining software that sits on top of existing systems such as SAP and Salesforce.
  • Processes drift from their designed path over time, and new products, acquisitions and customer expectations add pressure. Consultants used to find the problems slowly and expensively; the software finds them in four to eight weeks.
  • It covers accounts payable and receivable, procurement, order management, inventory and opportunity management, on-premises or as SaaS.
  • Deutsche Telekom Services Europe saved over €66 million in its procure-to-pay process (2 million order items and 9 million invoices a year) by finding duplicate payments and missed cash discounts.
  • Avnet freed more than €6 million of working capital by catching early supplier deliveries; Kraft Heinz cut overdue payments by 30% by rethinking cash discounts.
  • Execution management goes beyond finding problems to recommending and taking actions. Newer tools add a machine learning workbench, a natural-language process assistant (Process Copilot) built on a process query language, and a "digital twin" of the business's processes for AI and partner apps.

Ideas to apply

Building a new system is planned organisational change. Four kinds, from least to most risk and reward:

ChangeWhat it doesExample
AutomationHelps people do existing tasks fasterA roast-order screen replacing paper slips
Rationalisation of proceduresStreamlines standard operating procedures and removes bottlenecksOne product code list used by sales and the roastery
Business process redesignAnalyses, simplifies and redesigns whole processesCafés reorder automatically when their stock runs low
Paradigm shiftRethinks the nature of the business itselfSelling coffee as a subscription service

Business process management (BPM) is continuous improvement in five steps:

  1. Identify processes for change.
  2. Analyse the existing processes.
  3. Design the new process.
  4. Implement the new process.
  5. Measure continuously.

Process mining speeds up steps 2 and 5 by working from real event data instead of interviews and diagrams.

Questions

Q1

How can process mining give a business competitive advantage?

Operational excellence (lower costs, fewer duplicate payments, freed working capital), better customer service through smoother order handling, and faster, data-based decisions. Competitors still relying on slow consultant reviews improve more slowly.

Q2

Which companies benefit, and what should they consider before adopting it?

Large firms with high-volume, repeated processes and ERP or CRM systems that keep event logs: manufacturing, distribution, telecoms, retail, finance. Management: clear goals, executive support, budget, return on investment. Organisation: willingness to change processes and roles, staff training, process ownership. Technology: data quality and access to logs, integration with existing systems, cloud or on-premises, security and privacy of the data.

Q3

How does adding AI help?

AI handles huge volumes of process data, spots patterns and anomalies, predicts problems, recommends fixes, and lets non-technical staff ask questions in plain language. The process data in turn gives AI the business context it needs to be useful.

Cheat Sheet

T1 Strategy and the digital firm

Six objectivesOperational excellence · new products, services and business models · customer and supplier intimacy · improved decision making · competitive advantage · survival
What's newTechnology: cloud, big data, mobile, AI · Management: online collaboration, BI, virtual meetings · Organisations: social business, teleworking, co-created value
Digital firmRelationships and core processes digitally enabled · sense and respond · flexible · time shifting and space shifting

T1 What an IS is

IT = hardware + software + data management + networking (the platform)
IS = components that collect, process, store, distribute information for decisions and control
input → processing → output, with feedback · data (raw facts) → information (meaningful)
DimensionsOrganisation (people, structure, processes, politics, culture) · Management · Technology
LevelsSenior (strategic, long range) · Middle (programmes and plans) · Operational (day to day)
PerspectivesTechnical (computer science) · Behavioural (sociology, psychology, economics) · Sociotechnical (both)

MarketingInteractive marketing · targeted marketing (community, content, context, online behaviour) · SFA (inquiry → customise → commit → order)
ManufacturingCIM: simplify, automate, integrate, flexible · ERP/MRP, execution systems, engineering systems · CAM · five resources: materials, equipment, personnel, instructions, facilities
HRStrategic, tactical, operational · staffing, training and development, compensation
AccountingOrder processing · inventory control · receivable · payable · payroll · general ledger
FinanceCash management · investment management · capital budgeting · financial planning
TPS vs BITPS: routine, predefined questions · BI: non-routine decisions
EnterpriseERP inside · SCM suppliers · CRM customers · PRM partners · KMS employees
